# Course design

## Purpose

Teach beginner-to-low-intermediate Japanese for practical everyday conversation, including travel. Speaking and listening are the center of the course; reading and writing support those interactions. Kanafish should turn this course into short interactive practice, not replace it with a generic vocabulary or grammar syllabus.

## Functional categories

1. Greetings and social politeness
2. Introductions
3. Questions, answers and follow-up questions
4. Requests and transactions
5. Directions, time and plans
6. Preferences and recommendations
7. Simple explanations and stories
8. Problems, clarification and asking for help

## Beginner course route

The 12-week course is an original, independent companion broadly aligned to the scope and sequence of *GENKI I* (Third Edition), not a reproduction of its text, exercises, dialogues, or audio. It is grouped into four visible phases: **Meet people and find your first words** (weeks 1–3), **Describe, travel, and connect actions** (weeks 4–6), **Talk about people and share stories** (weeks 7–8), and **Make plans, share experiences, and get help** (weeks 9–12). The first phase assumes no prior Japanese. Every lesson states one practical goal and builds toward familiar everyday conversations with gradually less scripting. The course remains beginner-to-low-intermediate; advanced grammar and specialist vocabulary are left for later study.

The three-lesson rhythm is predictable: notice a scene and its meaning, practise a supported reply, then adapt the exchange. Phase names describe the learner's broader journey; the lesson rhythm describes what happens inside each week.

## Four-stage progression

1. **Recognize:** identify common phrases, understand the situation and notice a useful response.
2. **Respond with support:** use a short model, prompt or phrase bank to complete a predictable exchange.
3. **Adapt:** swap details, choose an appropriate phrase and ask or answer a follow-up.
4. **Communicate more freely:** handle a familiar situation with less scripting, including a misunderstanding or change of plan.

Each functional category should recur across stages. The learner should revisit useful language in new situations instead of seeing a phrase only once.

## Kana and kanji

- Introduce the complete core hiragana set in Week 1 and katakana in Week 2, alongside greetings, introductions, objects, and shopping. Treat these as first passes, not one-week mastery deadlines; practise a few groups per session and revisit them later. Add common voiced sounds, contracted combinations, small っ/ッ, and the katakana long-vowel mark in context.
- Introduce kana alongside the phrases and vocabulary learners actually say or hear. Short, repeated recognition and reading activities should reinforce each week's speaking goal, and the learner should use kana in the same conversation they are practising.
- Use kana as a pronunciation and reading aid, not as a reason to delay conversation practice.
- Teach katakana with useful travel words and loanwords (for example, menu and drink vocabulary).
- Use kanji selectively for practical recognition: include only characters or compounds that help in a particular section, such as **人 / 名前**, **円**, **駅**, **時間**, or **入口 / 出口**.
- Show a kana reading with unfamiliar kanji. Early goals should usually be recognition in context, not writing from memory or broad kanji coverage.
- Keep the script presentation consistent within each lesson and make the learner's reading support explicit. Whether to retain romaji as a temporary optional scaffold can be decided during the first pilot.

## Interactive lesson experience

A brand-new learner should first see a welcoming orientation and today's concrete goal—not a recall prompt that assumes a lesson happened. A revisit is offered only when the learner has completed source material, and it can always be skipped. With no eligible revisit, the lesson starts with new material. The scene's Japanese text is immediately readable, with meaning available on request; local audio and speech setup are optional and never gate progress.

A lesson should connect that short situation and dialogue to a small number of useful phrases, a response or comprehension activity, supported speaking practice, and a chance to adapt the exchange. Kana practice belongs in the same context. Hints and phrase banks can support early attempts and then recede as the learner retries with a changed detail.

Progress should make practice visible: lesson status, revisited activities, and learner-entered observations or evidence for the existing milestone checks. Completion is not a fluency score; the app should present the observable task criteria so learners can understand what they did and what to practise next.

## Evidence of learning

Assess what a learner can do in a short exchange and what they can understand from clear, beginner-level spoken Japanese. Prefer observable checks—successful requests, key details understood, follow-up questions and repair strategies—over abstract claims of fluency. The checkpoints in the [12-week outline](12-week-plan.md) are provisional and should be adjusted after piloting.

## Speech tools and assessment boundaries

The course includes optional local TTS for listen-first dialogue practice and Japanese course material, with slower replay, kana/meaning reveal, and ten Supertonic 3 voice presets that can stay fixed or rotate by week or lesson. Keep a tutor, partner or learner-led read-aloud available as an alternative; audio must not gate lesson progress. Supertonic 3 is currently the only enabled engine; other integrated engines are hidden until validated in the live setup. Speech recording, speech recognition and automated pronunciation assessment remain out of scope. Speaking and listening checks use task-level explanations and learner-entered evidence rather than machine-assigned proficiency scores.

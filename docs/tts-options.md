# Japanese TTS for Kanafish: options and a learner-first test

## Short answer

I could not identify a Japanese speech product named **“Phorus TTS”** from exact-name searches. The closest plausible name is **Fish Audio**: its current S2.1 model explicitly lists Japanese among its strongest languages and offers an API. “Phorus” alone mostly surfaced unrelated speaker products, so this is a likely spelling or hearing mix-up, not a confirmed identification.

For Kanafish, start a small side-by-side audition of **Amazon Polly Neural** and **Google Cloud Text-to-Speech Chirp 3 HD**. Both expose Japanese voices and controls useful for deliberate listening practice. Include **Fish Audio S2.1 Pro** in that audition if that is what you meant by “phorus”; its expressiveness is interesting, but the licensing distinction between its hosted API and self-hosted model weights needs special care. **No sample audition or independent Japanese quality test has been run for this note**, so the recommendation is about practical fit and published controls—not a claim that one voice sounds best.

## What matters for a conversation learner

A useful course voice should pronounce the lesson's Japanese accurately, sound clear at everyday speed, handle names/numbers and polite phrases consistently, and offer a slower replay without making slow speech the only version a learner hears. A natural voice matters, but clear phrasing and dependable pronunciation matter more than dramatic expressiveness for early listening.

A good later player flow is: **listen once at normal pace with text hidden → answer a gist/detail question → replay at a slower setting if wanted → reveal kana and meaning → replay by phrase**. Keep kana available beside speaking and listening. Do not use slowed audio as a substitute for normal-speed examples. For fairness, compare the same short test set across providers: greetings, polite questions, long vowels, small っ, numbers/prices, station and place names, and a two-speaker dialogue. Have a proficient Japanese speaker check pronunciation and a beginner judge intelligibility.

### How to read the costs

These are provider-published USD list prices reviewed on **17 November 2026**, before taxes and any account/region differences. Free allowances are conditional and can change. One million characters is far more than the short static dialogues in an early course pilot, so voice clarity, Japanese pronunciation, stable voice identity, and integration constraints likely matter more than a small price difference. Fish Audio bills by UTF-8 bytes and ElevenLabs by credits, so their headline figures are not a like-for-like per-character comparison.

## Recommendation

1. **Use Amazon Polly Neural as the first controlled baseline** for a small technical audition because it documents fine rate control and Japanese-specific pronunciation support. Test Takumi, Kazuha, and Tomoko rather than assuming a single default voice is best.
2. **Compare Google Chirp 3 HD** on the exact same clips for naturalness and learner intelligibility. Its multiple Japanese voices and published free allowance make it a useful quality comparison; retain it only if the difference is audible and valuable.
3. **Audition Fish Audio S2.1 Pro** if that is the product you had in mind. Check natural Japanese delivery on calm, everyday travel dialogue—not only expressive demos—and clarify API commercial terms before using it in a public app.
4. **Keep TTS behind a provider adapter** rather than binding lesson content to one vendor. A future interface can take locale, text, voice ID, rate, optional speaker/lexicon, and return audio plus any available timing marks. Keep credentials outside the Electron renderer; if mobile is supported, use a protected backend or a device voice rather than embedding a provider key in the app. Cache stable course audio by content/model/voice/rate and allow a generated-audio-free fallback.
5. **Do not implement speech recognition or speaking evaluation as part of this choice.** First validate listening activities and the human-readable can-do checks. A later TTS pilot can be limited to listen, replay, slow, and reveal-kana controls.
